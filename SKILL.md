# Circular Product Redesign

Use three distinct, connected layers: **the roadmap orders the work; strategies guide what to design; the hierarchy guides what to do with a particular unit or component.** Assess throughout: baseline at Step 0, decision support at Steps 1-5, reassessment at Step 6. Use the user's roadmap as the governing sequence and CIRCit as supporting assessment guidance.

## Operating defaults

- Preserve the initial delivered service, functions, comparable performance and use conditions unless the user explicitly accepts a change. Permit complete changes in architecture, form, materials, operating principle and business model.
- Apply the method to any physical product. Decompose durable assemblies, wear parts, consumables, packaging and supporting equipment; consider software/service dependencies where they determine useful life.
- Ask only unanswered decision-changing questions. Accept unknowns; use explicitly separate scenarios when needed, without routinely reconfirming the defaults.
- Present the real baseline diagnosis before concepts. Distinguish provided/observed/sourced facts, calculations, assumptions and unknowns. Do not infer hidden joints, grades, dimensions, failures or actual recovery from a photograph. Unknown evidence is not poor circularity.
- For a new product, mark the existing-product audit not applicable and define a service-equivalent benchmark; do not invent an existing product or claim improvement without a reference.
- Keep circularity, sustainability consequences, cost, engineering feasibility and confidence separate. Do not reward local sourcing, renewable materials or digital features without a demonstrated mechanism.
- Honor narrower requests. When the user asks for a full/end-to-end redesign, asks to use the full skill, or gives no stopping depth, default to comparable concept visuals, quantitative evaluation, final visualization, CAD-ready brief and checked conceptual STEP where feasible. Do not force CAD for service-only changes or narrower assessments.
- Label assumed geometry conceptual; omit or neutralize unresolved hazardous functional features. Never infer safety, compliance, manufacturability or production readiness from a render or STEP check.

## Three-layer framework

Read [references/framework.md](references/framework.md) and [references/process.md](references/process.md) for every full redesign.

Screen these exact six **design strategy axes**; their numbering is not a ranking:

1. **Narrow:** reduce material/resource demand per delivered function.
2. **Slow (long life):** increase durability and continued usefulness before replacement.
3. **Slow (life extension):** enable maintenance, repair, reuse, refurbishment and other suitable life-extending interventions.
4. **Close:** enable end-of-life materials to return to production through workable recovery routes.
5. **Regenerate:** investigate safe renewable/restorative resources and processes, with evidence rather than assumptions about ecological benefit.
6. **Inform:** supply usable information for operation, diagnosis, service and next-use decisions.

Apply **Maintain -> Repair -> Reuse -> Refurbish -> Remanufacture -> Recycle** to each relevant physical unit/component and its condition. Check the highest applicable route first and record evidence for accepting, skipping or rejecting it. Do not force every part through every route or treat the hierarchy as a calendar sequence. Design-stage routes are targets, not demonstrated outcomes.

Use the hierarchy as a value-retention preference, not proof of lowest environmental impact or a universal circularity score. Explain justified exceptions based on function, safety, remaining life, demand, processing and logistics. Report unavoidable losses outside the hierarchy. Identify repurposing or biological cycling as additional context-specific routes, not silent replacements of the six levels.

## Governing roadmap: preserve Steps 0-6

0. **If the product already exists: assess the current product.** Establish service/boundaries, component/lifecycle baseline, actual routes, evidence quality and initial circularity indicators. Separate any hypothetical reference scenario.
1. **Define a circular business model.** Couple concept architecture with ownership, value proposition, maintenance/spares, incentives, actors and retained-value economics.
2. **Design for disassembly (DfD).** Enable diagnosis, selective access, separation and reassembly for intended component routes; justify inapplicability and functional/safety trade-offs.
3. **Radical material simplification.** Challenge unnecessary mass, incompatible combinations, coatings and additives while preserving performance and higher-value loops. Do not impose mono-materiality.
4. **Use a digital product passport (DPP), where relevant.** Provide the minimum useful information system; justify a passport by actor/decision needs or verified applicable requirements.
5. **Engineer a reverse supply chain.** Specify collection, handling, inspection, routing, processing, destinations, ownership and costs; verify acceptance and demand. Allow maintenance/reuse without unnecessary returns.
6. **Evaluate and improve the redesign.** Compare baseline and every retained concept using common definitions; test sensitivity, select conditionally, validate and iterate.

Iterate when evidence changes while retaining the numbered roadmap in reports. Assess continuously; do not defer actor feasibility, information needs or route checks until the last step.

## Execution sequence within the roadmap

Make the user's deliverables explicit without inventing a competing numbered roadmap:

| Work phase | Roadmap placement | Required result for a full run |
|---|---|---|
| Assess the reference | Step 0 | Baseline functions, architecture, evidence and circularity measures |
| Generate distinct concepts | Start at Step 1; develop across Steps 2-5 | Different operating/architecture/loop solutions with stable concept IDs |
| Assess every concept | Throughout Steps 1-5; consolidate at Step 6 | Standard concept sheet: operation, component architecture, strategies, routes, measures, advantages, disadvantages and feasibility evidence |
| Explain concepts visually | After each architecture brief; before selection | Checked, comparable explanatory illustration for each retained concept, with assumptions and unresolved features identified |
| Compare and choose | Step 6 | Common comparison, sensitivity, gate status and recorded choice/rationale |
| Develop the chosen concept | Step 6 and iterations to affected earlier steps | Frozen design brief, parametric CAD, CAD-derived final views, STEP export and verification record |

Evaluate every generated concept initially. Record clearly rejected concepts with reasons; apply the full sheet, quantitative comparison and illustration requirements to every retained concept. Never omit a retained concept's disadvantages or feasibility because another concept scores higher. Follow the feasibility and visual-consistency checks in concepts-and-cad.md.

Record whether the selection is the user's choice, an explicitly delegated decision, or a provisional recommendation. Respect a request for the user to choose; otherwise continue the authorized full workflow with a clearly stated conditional selection. Do not silently replace a user-chosen concept; if it fails a mandatory requirement, explain the blocking issue and needed revision.

## Required traceability and comparison

Apply the complete output set below to a full redesign. For a preliminary or explicitly narrower request, use only the relevant subset, preserve the distinctions and evidence rules, and identify the next work without forcing tables, images, quantitative scores or CAD beyond that scope.

- Produce a **roadmap application table** for every retained concept, or one combined table with concept IDs: step | product-specific choice | strategy axis/axes and mechanism | component/condition and hierarchy route | evidence/status | measure, target and verification.
- Produce a six-axis **strategy-coverage matrix** across concepts. Mark applied, not applicable with reason, or unresolved. Do not force one concept per axis or award points for counting strategies.
- Produce a **component routing register**: component/condition | current route | intended highest feasible route | higher-route exclusions and evidence | actor/destination | acceptance test | fallback | confidence.
- Develop genuinely distinct architectures/loop systems, normally three to five with a justified smaller or larger set. Explain operation, functional equivalence, advantages, disadvantages, services, assumptions and trade-offs for each.
- Illustrate all retained concepts comparably before selection in a full run. Include additional equipment and consumables in the comparison boundary. Label generated views illustrative.
- Freeze functional unit, boundary, geography, time horizon, duty cycle, indicators and any scoring rules before calculation. Apply identical definitions to baseline and all concepts.
- Read [references/assessment.md](references/assessment.md). Use physical indicators first. Use MCI only with required data and its published method. Treat the internal CIRCit-aligned design score as a separate supplementary design-enabler measure, never actual circularity percentage, MCI or an ISO result.
- When calculating internal scores, use `scripts/compare_concepts.py --strict` for multiple concepts or `scripts/calculate_scores.py --strict` for a pair. These scripts rank design scores only; they do not compute MCI, model physical flows, validate routing or select a final design.
- Report units, denominators, sources, scenario, range and value status: measured, calculated, estimated, assumed, target or unknown. Test influential life, replacement, collection, yield, demand, logistics and any weighting assumptions.
- Prefer the strongest credible outcome supported by flows, useful life, retained function/value and route feasibility. Show conditional, tied or non-dominated choices when evidence cannot support a total order. Never rank solely by a strategy name, hierarchy label or aggregate score. Failed mandatory gates exclude concepts; unresolved gates permit conditional development only.

## Clear writing and transparent claims

- Use simple English by default, unless the user requests another language. Write short, direct sentences, usually with one main idea each.
- Keep technical terms when they improve precision, such as functional unit, disassembly, remanufacture and STEP. Briefly explain an unfamiliar term or acronym on first use; do not replace useful technical vocabulary with vague wording.
- Lead with the finding or decision. Add only the reason, evidence and next action needed to understand it. Avoid long introductions, repeated caveats, generic theory and unnecessary detail.
- Use short paragraphs for explanations, bullets for actions, and compact tables for comparing concepts. Keep images and labels easy to scan. Include technical calculations and traceability in supporting detail when needed, without hiding decision-critical evidence.
- Make transparency local and specific: label a value as measured, estimated, assumed, calculated, a target or unknown beside the value. State what is plausible, checked or still unverified beside the relevant feasibility claim, image or model.
- Explain only material limitations and their consequences. For example: "Illustrative view. Internal clearances are not checked." Or: "STEP import passed. Strength and assembly still need testing." Never promise zero hallucination or call a concept validated without the required evidence.
- Keep explanations brief without omitting disadvantages, feasibility limits, important assumptions or required comparisons. A short answer must still support the user's decision.
